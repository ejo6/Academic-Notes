
There is a special case of this kind of integral. Assume that $f(x,y)=g(x)\ h(y)$ and also asume we're integrating over rectangle $R=[a,b] \times[c,d]$. The integral becomes:

$$
\iint \limits_Rf(x,y)\ dA=\iint \limits_R g(x)\ h(y)\ dA=\int_c^d \int_a^b g(x)\ h(y)\ dx \ dy
$$
Note that it doesn't matter in this case which variable we integrate first as both arrive at the same conclusion. Next, notice that because the inner integral is with respect to $x$ and $h(y)$ is a function of only $y$ it can be considered a constant. 

$$
\iint \limits_Rf(x,y)\ dA=\iint \limits_R g(x)\ h(y)\ dA=\int_c^d h(y)\int_a^b g(x)\ dx \ dy
$$
This makes the second integral pretty easy to integrate, since it's a definite, its value is just a constant. It can be factored out to get:
$$
\iint \limits_Rf(x,y)\ dA=\iint \limits_R g(x)\ h(y)\ dA=\int_a^b g(x)\ dx\int_c^d h(y)\ dy
$$
Now we can **do each integral and multiply them together.** 

## Summary:
If $f(x,y)=g(x)\ h(y)$ and we are integrating over the rectangle $R=[a,b] \times[c,d]$ then, 
$$
\iint \limits_Rf(x,y)\ dA=\iint \limits_R g(x)\ h(y)\ dA=\int_a^b g(x)\ dx \cdot \int_c^d h(y)\ dy
$$
### Example:
$$
\iint \limits_R x\cos^2(y)\ dA=\int_{-2}^3x\ dx \cdot\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cos^2(y)\ dy
$$
$$
=(\frac{1}{2}x^2)|_{-2}^3\cdot \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}}1+\cos(2y)\ dy
=\frac{5}{2}(\frac{1}{2}(y+\frac{1}{2}\sin(2y)|_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}}))
=\frac{5\pi}{8}
$$

### Important Note
When looking at the following 2 integrals:
$$
\int x\sec^2(2y)+4xy\ dy
$$
$$
\int x^3-e^{-\frac{x}{y}}dx
$$
We need to **pay attention to the variable we're integrating over.** We also need to remember the **constant of integration are functions of the opposite variable.**
$$
\int x\sec^2(2y)+4xy\ dy=\frac{x}{2}\tan(2y)+2xy^2+g(x)
$$
$$
\int x^3-e^{-\frac{x}{y}}dx=\frac{1}{4}x^4+ye^{-\frac{x}{y}}+h(y)
$$
This is because if we differentiate the answer with respect to $x$ then any function of only $y$'s will differentiate to 0.
