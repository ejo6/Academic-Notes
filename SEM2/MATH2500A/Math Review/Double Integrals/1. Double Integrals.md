## Recall
An definite integral is really just the following:
$$
\int_a^b{f(x)}\ dx=\lim_{n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^n{f(x_i^*)\Delta x} 
$$
When we think about rectangles under a curve, $\Delta x$ is the width of a rectangle, and $f(x_i^*)$ is the height (function evaluated at midpoint of the rectangle).

## Estimating in 3 Dimensions
Now we want to integrate a function with 2 variables, $f(x, y)$. Functions with one var are integrated over an interval in 1 dimensional space, whereas it make sense that when integrating a function with 2 variables we integrate with an "base" in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (2 dimensional).

Assume $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a rectangle denoted as follows:
$$
R=[a,b] \times[c,d]
$$
This means that the ranges for $x$ and $y$ are $a \leq x \leq b$ and $c \leq y \leq d$. Also assuming for this examples that $f(x, y) \geq 0$ although not necessary. Think about a graph of surface $S$ which is a product of $f(x, y)$ over rectangle $R$.

To find the area bounded by vertical lines $(b, c), (a,c), (a,d), (b,d), R, S$ we can estimate like we would in $\mathbb{R}^1$. 

Divide up $a \leq x \leq b$ into $n$ subintervals and divide $c \leq y \leq d$ in $m$ subintervals. This cuts up $R$ into a series of smaller rectangles. Each of these has a **centre point** $(x_i^*, y_i^*) = \Delta A$. The height of these now rectangular prisms are $f(x_i^*, y_i^*)$. Sketch of both:

The volume is clearly $f(x_i^*, y_i^*)\cdot \Delta A$. The volume under surface $S$ is approximately:
$$
V\approx \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m f(x_i^*,y_j^*) \cdot \Delta A
$$
This is a double sum like:
```pseudocode
volume = 0

for i in range n:
	for i in range m:
		volume += (f(x, y) * deltaA)
```

Now, to get a better estimation of the volume, decrease the side of each rectangle:
$$
V=\lim_{n, m\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m f(x_i^*,y_j^*) \cdot \Delta A 
$$
This is very similar looking to the classic definite integral. This is the definition of a double integral, specifically of a function of two variables over a rectangle.

$$
\iint \limits_R f(x,y)\ dA=\lim_{n, m\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m f(x_i^*,y_j^*) \cdot \Delta A 
$$
The differential here is $dA$. $R$ below denotes the region were integrating over instead of having 4 bounds. 

## Computing in 2 Dimensions

Continue to assume $R= [a,b]\times [c,d]$. 

### Fubini's Theorem 
If $f(x,y)$ is continuous on $R= [a,b]\times [c,d]$ then,
$$
\iint \limits_R f(x,y)\ dA=\int_a^b\int_c^d f(x,y)\ dy\ dx=\int_c^d\int_a^b f(x,y)\ dx\ dy
$$
There are 2 ways of computing a definite integral over a rectangle. Make sure to watch the order of the differential. 
### Computing 
We compute the double integral by first computing 
$$
\int_c^df(x,y)\ dy.
$$
**We compute this by holding $x$ constant and integrating with respect to $y$ as if they're a single integral.**

### Examples
#### Example 1:
$$
\iint \limits_R 6xy^2\ dA, R=[2,4] \times [1,2]
$$
$$
\int_2^4\int_1^26xy^2\ dy\ dx=\int_2^4
[2xy^3]_{y=1}^{y=2} dx
$$
$$
\int_2^4
14x\ dx=[7x^2]_2^4=112-28=84
$$
#### Example 2:
$$
\iint \limits_R x^2y^2+cos(\pi x) +sin(\pi x) \ dA, R=[-2,-1] \times [0,1]
$$
$$
\int_0^1 \int_{-2}^{-1}x^2y^2+cos(\pi x) +sin(\pi x) \ dx\ dy
$$

$$
\int_0^1[\frac{1}{3}x^3y^2+\frac{1}{\pi} \sin(\pi x)+x \sin(\pi y)]_{-2}^{-1}
$$
Note that:
$$
\int \sin(\pi y)\ dx=\sin (\pi y)\cdot\int1\ dx=x \cdot \sin(\pi y)
$$
Continuing on:
$$
=\int_0^1 \frac{7}{3}y^2+\sin(\pi y)\ dy=\frac{7}{9}y^3-\frac{1}{\pi}\cos(\pi y)|_0^1
$$
$$
=\frac{7}{9}+\frac{2}{\pi}
$$
Deciding whether to integrate with respects to $x$ or $y$ first can dramatically change the difficulty of the problem, and is an important step.
