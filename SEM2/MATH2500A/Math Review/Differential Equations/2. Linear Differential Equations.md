The first (and very simple) case of interest is first order differential equations. For first order differential equations, we can actually derive a formula for the general solution. The general solution is derived below. Recall the goal is to arrive at a solution in form $y=y(t)$. A first order differential equation will need to be in form
$$
\frac{dy}{dt}+p(t)y=g(t)
$$
Where $p(t)$ and $g(t)$ are continuous functions. Now assume there is a function $\mu(t)$ called an **integrating factor**. $\mu(t)$ will be derived after the general solution.

1. Multiply everything by $\mu(t)$
$$
\mu(t)\frac{dy}{dt}+\mu(t)\ p(t)y=\mu(t)\ g(t)
$$
2. Assume that $\mu(t)\ p(t)=\mu'(t)$, and sub in 
$$
\mu(t)\frac{dy}{dt}+\mu'(t)y=\mu(t)\ g(t)
$$
3. Realize that the left side is just a product rule $\mu(t)\frac{dy}{dt}+\mu'(t)y=\mu(t)y'+\mu'(t)y=(\mu(t)\ p(t))'$
$$
(\mu(t)\ p(t))'=\mu(t)\ g(t)
$$
4. Integrate both sides
$$
\int(\mu(t)\ y(t))'dt=\int\mu(t)\ g(t)\ dt
$$
$$
\mu(t)\ y(t)+c=\int\mu(t)\ g(t)\ dt
$$
$$
y(t)=\frac{\int\mu(t)\ g(t)\ dt-c}{\mu(t)}\Rightarrow y(t)=\frac{\int\mu(t)\ g(t)\ dt+c}{\mu(t)}
$$
Now we want to go back to determine if this general solution is correct. Recall $\mu(t)\ p(t)=\mu'(t)$. 

Divide both sides by $\mu(t)$ 
$$
\frac{\mu'(t)}{\mu(t)}=p(t)\Rightarrow (\ln\mu(t))'=p(t)
$$
Integrate both sides
$$
\ln(\mu(t))+k=\int p(t)\ dt
$$
$$
\ln(\mu(t))=\int p(t)\ dt+k
$$
We use $k$ here to avoid confusion with c. Exponentiate both sides to get rid of the natural log
$$
\mu(t)=e^{\int p(t)\ dt+k}=e^k e^{\int p(t)\ dt}
$$
Since $e^k$ is still an unknown constant, it can just be $k$ again
$$
\mu(t)=k e^{\int p(t)\ dt}
$$
We now have a formula for the general solution, and for the integrating factor. Since we have two unknown constants, this will cause trouble later on. We want to try and eliminate one.

Sub in the integrating factor to the general solution:
$$
y(t)=\frac{\int k e^{\int p(t)\ dt}\ g(t)\ dt+c}{k e^{\int p(t)\ dt}}
$$
$$
y(t)=\frac{k\int e^{\int p(t)\ dt}\ g(t)\ dt+c}{k e^{\int p(t)\ dt}}
$$
$$
y(t)=\frac{\int e^{\int p(t)\ dt}\ g(t)\ dt+\frac{c}{k}}{ e^{\int p(t)\ dt}}
$$
Simplify $\frac{c}k$ to just $c$. Giving us:
$$
y(t)=\frac{\int \mu(t)\ dt+c}{\mu(t)}
$$
Where,
$$
\mu(t)=e^{\int p (t)\ dt}
$$
## Solution Process
The solution process for a fist order linear differential equation is as follows.
1. Put the differential equation in the initial form $\frac{dy}{dt}+p(t)y=g(t)$
2. Find the integrating factor $\mu(t)$, using $\mu(t)=e^{\int p (t)\ dt}$ 
3. Multiply everything in the differential equation by $\mu(t)$ and verify the left side becomes the product rule $(\mu(t)\ y(t))'$ and write it as such. 
4. Integrate both sides, make sure you properly deal with the constant of integration
5. Solve for the solution $y(t)$ 

## Examples
#### Example 1
Find the solution to the following differential equation
$$
\frac{dv}{dt}=9.8-0.196v 
$$
Step 1.
$$
\frac{dv}{dt}+0.196v=9.8 
$$
Step 2. 
$$
p(t)=0.196,\ \ \ \ \mu(t)=e^{\int0.196dt}=e^{0.196t}
$$
Step 3.
$$
e^{0.196t}\cdot \frac{dv}{dt}+e^{0.196t}\cdot 0.196v=9.8\cdot e^{0.196t}
$$
The left side can clearly be simplified to a power rule
$$
(e^{0.196t}\cdot v)=9.8\cdot e^{0.196t}
$$
Step 4.
$$
\int (e^{0.196t}\cdot v)\ dt=\int9.8\cdot e^{0.196t}\ dt
$$
$$
e^{0.196t}\cdot v+k=50\cdot e^{0.196t}+c
$$
$$
v=50+(c-k)e^{-0.196t}
$$
$$
v(t)=50+ce^{-0.196t}
$$
#### Example 2
Solve the following IVP
$$
\frac{dv}{dt}=9.8-0.196v\ \ \ \ v(0)=48
$$
Get the solution from [[#Example 1]]: $v(t)=50+ce^{-0.196t}$ 
$$
48=50+ce^{-0.196(0)}\ \ \ \ c=-2
$$
Solution is 
$$
v(t)=50-2e^{-0.196t}
$$
#### Example 6
Find the solution to the IVP and determine all possible behaviours of the solution as $t\to \infty$ . If this behaviour depends on the value of $y_0$ give this dependence.
$$
2y'-y=4\sin(3t)\ \ \ \ y(0)=y_0
$$
Step 1. We need a factor of 1 in front of $y'$ 
$$
y'-\frac{1}2y=2\sin(3t)
$$
Step 2.
$$
\mu(t)=e^{\int-1/2\ dt}=e^{-1/2t}
$$
Step 3. 
$$
e^{-1/2t}\cdot y'-e^{-1/2t}\cdot \frac{1}2y=2\sin(3t)\cdot e^{-1/2t}
$$
$$
(e^{-1/2t}\cdot y)'=2\sin(3t)\cdot e^{-1/2t}
$$
Step 4.
$$
e^{-1/2t}\cdot y=\int2\sin(3t)\cdot e^{-1/2t}\ dt
$$
Ignore the integration by parts for now
$$
e^{-1/2t}\cdot y=\frac{24}{37}\cos(3t)-\frac{4}{37}\sin(3t)+ce^{t/2}
$$
Apply the initial condition to find c
$$
y_0=y(0)=-\frac{24}{37}+c\Rightarrow c=y_0+\frac{24}{37}
$$
The solution is
$$
y(t)=-\frac{24}{37}\cos(3t)-\frac{4}{37}\sin(3t)+(y_0+\frac{24}{37})e^{t/2}
$$
The first to terms are just creating an oscillating wave. The third erm of $e^{t/2}$ dictates how the wave increases or decreases. When $c < 0,\ y(t)\to-\infty$, $c >0,\ y(t)\to\infty$, $c =0,\ y(t)\ \text{remains finite}$. 

Just  apply this to terms of $y_0$.  $c < -\frac{24}{37},\ y(t)\to-\infty$, $c >-\frac{24}{37},\ y(t)\to\infty$, $c =-\frac{24}{37},\ y(t)\ \text{remains finite}$.
