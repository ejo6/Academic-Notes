A differential equation is any equation which contains derivatives, either ordinary or partial. A classic example: If an object of mass $m$ is moving with acceleration $a$ and being acted on with force $F$ then,
$$
F=ma
$$
This is a differential equation, although it needs some rewriting. Acceleration $a$ could be
$$
a=\frac{dv}{dt} \text{ or } a=\frac{d^2u}{dt^2}
$$
Where $v$ is the velocity of an object and $u$ is the position function of the object at time $t$. Recall $f'(a)=v$ and $f''(v)=u$. $F$ may also be a function of time.

We can rewrite the law as a differential equation either in terms of velocity or position,
$$
m\frac{dv}{dt}=F(t,v)
$$
$$
m\frac{d^2u}{dt^2}=F(t,v,\frac{du}{dt})
$$
So $m \times$ either the first or second derivative of $a$.

Differential equations can take many forms, such as
$$
ay''+by'+cy=g(t)
$$
$$
\frac{\partial^3u}{\partial x^2 \partial t}=1+\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}
$$
## Rules
1. The **order** of a differential equation is the largest derivative present in the equation. $order(f''(x)) = 2$, $order(\partial^3 u / \partial x^2)=3$
2. A differential equation is **ordinary (ode)** if it only has ordinary derivatives in it, and **partial (pde)** if it has any partial derivatives in it. 
3. A **linear** differential equation is any differential equation that can be written in the form
$$
a_n(t)y^{(n)}{(t)}+a_{n-1}(t)y^{(n-1)}{(t)}+...+a_1(t)y'{(t)}+a_0(t)y{(t)}=g(t)
$$
	3.1. Functions of $t$ can be whatever you want
	3.2. No products of $y$ with each other. $y\cdot y'$ or $(y')^2 \cdot y''$ are not allowed. Each derrivative stands alone, only multiplied by a coefficient dependent on $t$.
	3.3. No powers other than 1, and nothing inside another function:
	- $y^3$
	- $\sqrt{y'}$ 
	- $e^y$ 
	- $\sin(y)$ 
	are all not allowed.

## Linear Differential Equations
### Valid Solutions
A **solution** to a differential equation on interval $\alpha < t <\beta$ is any function $y(t)$ which satisfies the differential equation in question on interval $\alpha < t < \beta$. 
#### Example:
Show that $y(x)=x^{-\frac{3}{2}}$ is a solution to $4x^2 y''+12xy'+3y=0$ for $x>0$. 

We'll need the first and second derivative to do this
$$
y'(x)=-\frac{3}{2}x^{-\frac{5}{2}}\ \ \ \ y''(x)=-\frac{15}{4}x^{-\frac{7}{2}}
$$
Plug these into the differential equation
$$
4x^2 (-\frac{15}{4}x^{-\frac{7}{2}})+12x(-\frac{3}{2}x^{-\frac{5}{2}})+3(x^{-\frac{3}{2}})=0
$$
$$
15x^{-\frac{3}{2}}-18x^{-\frac{3}{2}}y(x)+3x^{-\frac{3}{2}}=0
$$
$$
0=0
$$
So, $y(x)=x^{-\frac{3}{2}}$ did satisfy the equation. 
### Initial Condition(s)
**Initial Condition(s)** are conditions on the solution which allows us to determine which solution we're actually after. i.c.'s are of the form
$$
y(t_0)=y_0\ \ \ \ \text{and/or}\ \ \ \ y^k(t_0)=y_k
$$

### Initial Value Problem
An **IVP** is a differential equation along with an appropriate number of initial conditions. 2 examples:
$$
4x^2y''+12xy'+3y=0 \ \ \ \ y(4)=\frac{1}8, \ \ y'(4)=-\frac{3}{64}
$$
$$
2ty'+4y=3
$$
### Interval of Validity
The **interval of validity** for an IVP with initial conditions(s)
$$
y(t_0)=y_0\ \ \ \ \text{and/or}\ \ \ \ y^k(t_0)=y_k
$$
is the largest possible interval on which the solution is valid and contains $t_0$. Easy to define, difficult to define.

### General Solution
The **general** solution to a differential equation is the most general forms of the solution that can take and doesn't take any initial conditions into account. For example:
$$
y(t)=\frac{3}4+\frac{c}{t^2}
$$
is the general solution to:
$$
2ty'+4y=3
$$
### Actual Solution
The **actual solution** to a differential equation is the specific solution that satisfies the equation and initial conditions. 

#### Example
What is the actual solution to the following IVP?
$$
2ty'+4y=3 \ \ \ \ y(1)=-4
$$
From the previous example, $y(t)=\frac{3}4+\frac{c}{t^2}$ is the general solution to $2ty'+4y=3$. 
$$
-4=y(1)=\frac{3}4+\frac{c}{1^2} \Rightarrow c=-\frac{19}4
$$
So the actual solution to the IVP is:
$$
y(t)=\frac{3}4-\frac{19c}{4t^2}
$$
### Implicit/Explicit Solution 
An **explicit solution** is any solution that is given in the form $y=y(t)$. In other words, the only place that $y$ shows up is once on the left side and only raised to the first power. An implicit solution is any solution that's not in explicit form. Both can be general. Example:

#### Example (implicit)
$y^2=t^2 -3$ is the actual implicit solution to $y'=\frac{t}{y},\ \ \ \ y(2) =-1$ 

$$
y'=\frac{t}{y}\Rightarrow \frac{dy}{dt}=\frac{t}{y}
$$
$$
y\cdot dy=t\cdot dt
$$
Integrate both sides:
$$
\int y\ dy=\int t\ dt
$$
$$
\frac{y^2}{2}=\frac{t^2}{2}+C \Rightarrow y^2=t^2+C
$$
Sub in the initial value:
$$
-1^2=2^2-C,\ \ \ \ C=-3
$$
Thus $y^2=t^2 -3$ is the actual implicit solution to $y'=\frac{t}{y},\ \ \ \ y(2) =-1$ 
#### Example (explicit)
Find an actual explicit solution to $y'=\frac{t}{y},\ \ \ \ y(2) =-1$.

We already know from the previous example that an implicit solution to this IVP is $y^2=t^2 -3$. To find the explicit solution all we need to do is solve for $y(t)$. 

Take the square root of both sides:
$$
y(t)=\pm \sqrt{t^2-3}
$$

Since this is actually 2 functions, sub in and see what satisfies the initial condition. The negative one does.
$$
y(t)=-\sqrt{t^2-3}
$$

It should be noted that **it is not always possible** to find an explicit solution
