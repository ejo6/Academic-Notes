## Big Picture

- **Recap:** supervised learning searches for a function $f$ that maps inputs $\boldsymbol{x}$ to outputs $y$. Linear regression picks $f(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top \phi(\boldsymbol{x})$ and fits $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ by minimizing a loss such as the mean squared error (MSE).
- "Linear" means linear **with respect to $\boldsymbol{\theta}$**, not necessarily in $\boldsymbol{x}$. The feature map $\phi(\boldsymbol{x})$ can be non-linear (e.g. polynomial features), and the model is still "linear regression".
- **Today's question:** _why_ the squared error? Is there a principled reason to pick one loss over another?
- **Answer:** treat learning as probabilistic inference. Assume the data was generated by a probability distribution with unknown parameters, then choose the parameters that make the observed data most probable (**maximum likelihood estimation, MLE**). Under a Gaussian noise assumption, this turns out to be _exactly_ minimizing squared error.

> [!tip] Why this matters 
> This is the recipe used for almost every model in the course (and in modern ML, including neural networks and LLMs): pick a model, pick a probability distribution for the outputs, maximize the likelihood. Different distributions give different losses. Squared error is just the Gaussian special case.

## Probability Refresher

### Sample Space, Events, and Probability

- **Sample space** $\Omega$: the set of all possible outcomes you might observe.
    - Coin flip: $\Omega = \lbrace\text{Head}, \text{Tail}\rbrace$
    - Die roll: $\Omega = \lbrace1,2,3,4,5,6\rbrace$
    - Person's height in cm: $\Omega = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$
    - Height (cm) and number of siblings together: $\Omega = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} \times \mathbb{N}$ (a Cartesian product, so each outcome is a pair)
- An **event** is a _subset_ of $\Omega$ (a collection of outcomes we might care about).
    - Coin: $\lbrace\text{Head}\rbrace$, $\lbrace\text{Tail}\rbrace$, $\varnothing$, or $\Omega$ itself
    - Height at least 190 cm: $[190, \infty)$
- **Probability** $P$ assigns each event a number measuring how likely it is.
    - $P(\lbrace\text{Head}\rbrace) = 0.5$, $P(\lbrace\text{Head},\text{Tail}\rbrace) = 1$
    - $P([190,\infty)) = 0.01$ (illustrative number: about 1% of people are at least 190 cm)

> [!note] Modelling caveat 
> Real heights are bounded, but we model them on $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$) because it is mathematically convenient. Later, a Gaussian will assign a tiny but non-zero probability to absurd heights. All models are approximations.

### Probability Spaces

A **probability space** is a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$:

- $\Omega$ is the sample space.
- $\mathcal{F}$ is the set of all events we are allowed to ask about. (Formally a $\sigma$-algebra. For finite or countable $\Omega$ you can just think of it as _all_ subsets.)
- $P$ is the probability measure, satisfying the **axioms of probability**:

1. $P(A) \geq 0$ for all $A \in \mathcal{F}$ (no negative probabilities).
2. $P(\Omega) = 1$ and $P(\varnothing) = 0$ (something must happen; the impossible event has probability zero).
3. If $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F}$ are **disjoint** (no overlap), then $$ P\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n\right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} P(A_n) $$ This is _additivity_: probabilities of non-overlapping events simply add.

**Example (coin flip):** $\Omega = \lbrace\text{Head},\text{Tail}\rbrace$ and $\mathcal{F} = \lbrace\varnothing, \lbrace\text{Head}\rbrace, \lbrace\text{Tail}\rbrace, \Omega\rbrace$, with $P(\varnothing)=0$, $P(\lbrace\text{Head}\rbrace)=0.5$, $P(\lbrace\text{Tail}\rbrace)=0.5$, $P(\Omega)=1$. Note axiom 3 is consistent here: $P(\lbrace\text{Head}\rbrace) + P(\lbrace\text{Tail}\rbrace) = 1 = P(\Omega)$.

Useful consequences of the axioms (not on the slides, but used constantly):

- Complement rule: $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$.
- Inclusion-exclusion: $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$.

### Random Variables

**Motivation:** we often want to do arithmetic on outcomes. "Head + Head" is meaningless, so we cannot count heads in $N$ flips directly. A **random variable** is a function that converts outcomes into numbers.

- Define $X : \Omega \to \lbrace 0,1 \rbrace$ by: $X(\omega) = 1$ if $\omega = \text{Head}$, and $X(\omega) = 0$ if $\omega = \text{Tail}$.
- Compact form using an indicator function: $X(\omega) = \mathbb{1}[\omega = \text{Head}]$.
- Each flip yields an outcome $\omega$, and we record $x = X(\omega)$. After $N$ flips we have $x_1, \dots, x_N$, and the number of heads is simply $\sum_{n=1}^{N} x_n$.

**Example (GPA):** $\Omega = \lbrace \text{A},\text{B},\text{C},\text{D},\text{F} \rbrace$ and the mapping is $X(\text{A}) = 4$, $X(\text{B}) = 3$, $X(\text{C}) = 2$, $X(\text{D}) = 1$, $X(\text{F}) = 0$.

#### Discrete vs. Continuous

- **Discrete random variable:** $X : \Omega \to S$ where $S$ is a **countable** set (finite, or countably infinite like $\mathbb{N}$). Coin flip: $S=\lbrace0,1\rbrace$.
- **Continuous random variable:** $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$. Height: $X(\omega) = \omega$.

> [!question] Slide question: discrete or continuous? Height with $\Omega = \mathbb{R}$ and $X(\omega) = \mathbb{1}[\omega \geq 190]$, which equals $1$ if $\omega \geq 190$ and $0$ otherwise.
> 
> **Answer: discrete.** Even though the underlying outcomes $\omega$ are continuous, $X$ only takes values in $\lbrace0,1\rbrace$, a countable set. What matters is the _range_ of $X$, not the sample space. The indicator $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ is $1$ when its condition is true and $0$ otherwise.

### Probability Distributions

- Given $X : \Omega \to S$ (discrete or continuous), the notation $X \in A$ means the event $\lbrace\omega \in \Omega \text{ such that } X(\omega) \in A\rbrace$: all outcomes that $X$ maps into $A$.
- $P(X \in A)$ for $A \subseteq S$ is the **distribution** of $X$.
- Common shorthands:
    - $P(X = x) = P(X \in \lbrace x\rbrace)$
    - $P(X > x) = P(X \in (x,\infty))$
    - $P(x < X \leq y) = P(X \in (x,y])$

**Example (GPA):** $S = \lbrace0,1,2,3,4\rbrace$. $P(X \in \lbrace3,4\rbrace)$ is the probability of getting a 3 or a 4, and $P(X > 2)$ is the probability of a GPA above 2 (which is the same event as $\lbrace3,4\rbrace$).

### Probability Mass Functions (PMF), Discrete Case

The PMF $p_X(x)$ is defined so that probabilities of sets are sums over points: $$ P(X \in A) = \sum_{x \in A} p_X(x) \quad \text{for any } A \subseteq S $$

- It is defined on **individual points** $x$ rather than on sets $A$.
- **Properties:** $p_X(x) \geq 0$ for all $x \in S$, and $\sum_{x \in S} p_X(x) = 1$.
- Here $p_X(x) = P(X = x)$ is an honest probability.
- GPA example: $P(X \in \lbrace3,4\rbrace) = p_X(3) + p_X(4)$.
- If the context is clear, write $p(x)$, $p(y)$, etc. instead of $p_X(x)$.

### Probability Density Functions (PDF), Continuous Case

For a continuous $X$, probabilities of sets are **integrals** of the PDF: $$ P(X \in A) = \int_{A} p_X(x)\ \mathrm{d}x \quad \text{for any } A \subseteq \mathbb{R} $$

- **Properties:** $p_X(x) \geq 0$ for all $x$, and $\int_{\mathbb{R}} p_X(x)\ \mathrm{d}x = 1$.
- Extends to vectors: replace $\mathbb{R}$ with $\mathbb{R}^D$.
- The slide's plot shows a bell-shaped height density peaking near 165 cm with maximum value about $0.043$.

> [!warning] A density is NOT a probability
> 
> - $p_X(x)$ can be **greater than 1** (a very narrow distribution has a tall peak). Only the _area_ under the curve must be at most 1.
> - For a continuous variable, $P(X = x) = 0$ for any single point. Only intervals have non-zero probability: $P(a < X \leq b) = \int_a^b p_X(x)\ \mathrm{d}x$.
> - $p_X(x)$ is better read as "probability per unit length near $x$". Bigger density means "more likely region", which is exactly what we need for likelihoods later.

|                      | Discrete (PMF)        | Continuous (PDF)           |
| -------------------- | --------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Value at a point     | An actual probability | Density, not a probability |
| Probability of a set | Sum over the set      | Integral over the set      |
| Total                | Sums to 1             | Integrates to 1            |
| Can exceed 1?        | No                    | Yes                        |

### Joint Distributions

- For two random variables $X$ and $Y$, the **joint PMF/PDF** is $p_{XY}(x,y)$, describing them _together_.
- Discrete example: $p_{XY}(1,1)$ is the probability of the coin flip sequence Head, Head.
- Continuous example (slide): a contour plot of $p(x,y)$ for height $x$ and weight $y$. The bright yellow centre (around 165 cm, 70 kg) is the most likely combination, and the tilted ellipse shows that taller people tend to be heavier, i.e. $X$ and $Y$ are **dependent**.

### Marginal and Conditional Distributions

**Marginal** (ignore one variable by summing/integrating it out): $$ p_X(x) = \sum_{y} p_{XY}(x,y) \qquad \text{or} \qquad p_X(x) = \int p_{XY}(x,y)\ \mathrm{d}y $$ Intuition: squash the 2D contour plot onto the height axis, adding up all the weights for each height.

**Conditional** (distribution of $X$ once we _know_ $Y = y$): $$ p_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{p_{XY}(x,y)}{p_Y(y)} $$ Intuition: take a slice of the joint plot at a fixed weight $y$, then divide by $p_Y(y)$ so the slice integrates to 1 again.

Rearranging gives the **product rule**, $p_{XY}(x,y) = p_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y)\ p_Y(y)$. Supervised learning is really about the conditional $p(y \mid \boldsymbol{x})$: "given the input, what is the distribution of the output?"

### I.I.D. Random Variables

- $X_1$ and $X_2$ are **independent** if their joint factorizes: $$ p_{X_1 X_2}(x_1, x_2) = p_{X_1}(x_1)\ p_{X_2}(x_2) \quad \text{for all } x_1, x_2 $$
- They are **identically distributed** if they share the same $p_X(x)$.
- If $\lbrace X_1,\dots,X_N\rbrace$ are **i.i.d.** (independent and identically distributed) with PDF/PMF $p_X(x)$: $$ p(x_1,\dots,x_N) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} p_X(x_n) $$

**Example:** $X_n$ = height of the $n$-th random person in the class. It is reasonable to treat them as i.i.d. with a common PDF $p(x)$, so the probability (density) of seeing $x_1 = 172, x_2 = 152, \dots, x_N = 184$ is the product $\prod_n p(x_n)$.

> [!note] i.i.d. is an assumption
>  It is convenient, and it is what makes the likelihood factor into a product, but it can be wrong. Siblings in the same class have correlated heights, and consecutive measurements in a time series (like the gym weight-room counts from the recap slide) are not independent. Always ask whether i.i.d. is reasonable for your data.

### Expectation

The **expectation** (mean) of $X$ with PMF/PDF $p(x)$: $$ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{x} x\ p(x) \qquad \text{or} \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\ p(x)\ \mathrm{d}x $$ It is a probability-weighted average of all possible values.

**Computing it by sampling (Monte Carlo):**

1. Draw samples $x_1,\dots,x_N$ from $p(x)$. Notation: $x_n \sim p(x)$.
2. Average them: $$ \mathbb{E}[X] \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} x_n $$ This works because of the law of large numbers: the sample average converges to the true mean as $N$ grows. Height example: $\mathbb{E}[X] = 170$ means the average height of a random person is 170 cm, and we could estimate it by measuring many people.

### Variance

$$ \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\right] $$ The standard deviation is $\sigma(X) = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}$.

- Variance is the **average squared deviation from the mean**. Its units are squared (cm$^2$), which is why we also use the standard deviation, which is back in the original units (cm).
- Height example with $\mathbb{E}[X] = 170$: $\mathrm{Var}(X) = 100\ \text{cm}^2$ means $\sigma(X) = 10$ cm, i.e. typical heights are about 10 cm from 170 cm.
- Handy equivalent form: $\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$.

### Gaussian (Normal) Distribution

A continuous random variable $X$ is **Gaussian** if it has PDF $$ p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} e^{\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)} = \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) $$

- $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$ is the **mean parameter** (centre of the bell). In fact $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu$.
- $\sigma^2$ is the **variance parameter** (width of the bell), and $\mathrm{Var}(X)=\sigma^2$. It must be strictly positive for the density to be defined (the slide writes $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$).
- This is a **parametric PDF** $p(x \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$ with parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\mu, \sigma^2)$. The distribution's whole shape is pinned down by a handful of numbers, which we can then _learn_ from data.
- Key intuition: density is highest at $x = \mu$ and falls off with squared distance $(x-\mu)^2$. This squared distance is the seed of the squared-error loss later.

## Likelihood Functions

### The Likelihood

- Let $X$ have a parametric PMF/PDF $p(x \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$.
- In practice we **do not know the true $\boldsymbol{\theta}$**, but we do have observed data $x_n$.
- So we flip our viewpoint: fix the data $x_n$ and treat $p(x_n \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$ as a **function of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$**. This is called the **likelihood**.
- Interpretation: _"If I assume this particular $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, how likely is my observation $x_n$?"_

> [!warning] Likelihood is not a distribution over $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ As a function of $x$, $p(x \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$ integrates to 1. As a function of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, it generally does **not**, and it is not "the probability that $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is correct". It only scores how well each candidate $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ explains the data. (Turning it into a probability over $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ requires Bayes' rule and a prior, which is a different topic.)

#### Worked example: height

Assume height is $\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2)$ and you observe someone who is $x_n = 210$ cm tall.

- **$\mu = 10$, $\sigma^2 = 1$:** the observation is 200 standard deviations from the mean. The density is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-20000}$, effectively zero. This $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is a terrible explanation.
- **$\mu = 178$, $\sigma^2 = 100$:** $\sigma = 10$, so 210 cm is $3.2\sigma$ away. Density $= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot 10}\ e^{-32^2/200} \approx 0.0399 \times 0.0060 \approx 2.4\times 10^{-4}$. Unlikely but plausible: tall people exist.
- **$\mu = 210$, $\sigma^2 = 1$:** the observation sits exactly at the mean, so the density is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.399$, the largest of the three by far. For this single observation it is the best explanation.

Takeaway: the likelihood is high when the parameters place a lot of density _where the data actually is_.

### Log-Likelihood

Suppose the dataset $\mathcal{D} = \lbrace x_1,\dots,x_N\rbrace$ is i.i.d. from $p(x \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$. The likelihood of the whole dataset is the product of individual likelihoods, and the log turns it into a sum (using $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$): $$ p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} p(x_n \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \iff \log p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \log p(x_n \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) $$

Why work with the log?

- $\log$ is **strictly increasing**, so the $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ that maximizes the likelihood also maximizes the log-likelihood.
- Products of many numbers less than 1 **underflow** to zero on a computer. Sums of logs are numerically stable.
- Sums are much easier to differentiate than products.

### Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

$$ \boldsymbol{\theta}^{*} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \log p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) $$ **MLE picks the parameters that make the observed dataset most probable.** Equivalent view: minimize the **negative log-likelihood (NLL)**, which is the "loss" in this framework.

#### Example: coin toss

Encode Head $=1$, Tail $=0$ and let $\theta = P(\text{Head})$. The single-flip distribution (a **Bernoulli**) is $$ p(x \mid \theta) = \theta^{x}(1-\theta)^{1-x} $$ For the slide's dataset $\mathcal{D}=\lbrace0,1,1,0\rbrace$: $p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta) = \theta^2(1-\theta)^2$, so $\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta) = 2\log\theta + 2\log(1-\theta)$. This is the arch-shaped curve on the slide, peaking at $\theta = 0.5$ with value $4\log 0.5 \approx -2.77$.

**General result:** with $k$ heads in $N$ flips, $\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta) = k\log\theta + (N-k)\log(1-\theta)$. Setting the derivative to zero: $$ \frac{k}{\theta} - \frac{N-k}{1-\theta} = 0 \implies \theta^{*} = \frac{k}{N} $$ So MLE for a coin is just the **fraction of heads**. Reassuringly intuitive.

#### Varying the dataset (slide)

- **$\mathcal{D}=\lbrace5 \text{ Heads}\rbrace$:** $\log p = 5\log\theta$ is increasing, so the maximum is at the boundary $\theta^{*}=1$. MLE concludes "this coin always lands Heads", which is an **overconfident** conclusion from little data (a classic weakness of MLE with small samples).
- **$\mathcal{D}=\lbrace0\rbrace$:** $\log p = \log(1-\theta)$ is decreasing, so $\theta^{*}=0$. Same overconfidence, mirrored.
- **Third plot:** the slide caption says "9 Heads, 1 Tail", but the curve peaks near $\theta = 0.1$ at about $-3.25$. That matches **1 Head and 9 Tails**, since $\log(0.1) + 9\log(0.9) \approx -3.25$. With 9 Heads and 1 Tail the peak would be at $\theta^{*}=0.9$. This looks like a labelling slip on the slide, so it is worth confirming in lecture or lab.

Pattern to remember: more (and more balanced) data gives a sharper, more believable peak.

#### Example: which Gaussian fits best?

The slide shows 50 height samples (crosses, spread roughly from 148 to 188 cm) against four candidate Gaussians:

- $\mu=170, \sigma^2=100$ (blue): centred on the data with about the right spread. **Best of the four.**
- $\mu=140, \sigma^2=100$ (orange): centred too far left, so most data points sit in the far tail where density is near zero.
- $\mu=170, \sigma^2=900$ (green): right centre, but far too wide. Probability mass is spread thinly over places with no data, so density at each point is low.
- $\mu=155, \sigma^2=225$ (red): shifted left and wider than needed. Better than orange or green in places but still not matched to the data.

MLE automates this: it searches over $(\mu,\sigma^2)$ for the curve with the largest total log-density on the data.

**Closed-form Gaussian MLE** (a gap-filler, and likely what the lab uses). Write the log-likelihood as $-\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n}(x_n-\mu)^2$ and set its derivatives to zero: $$ \hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} x_n \qquad \left(\sigma^{2}\right)^{2} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(x_n-\mu^{*})^2 $$ The MLE mean is the sample mean, and the MLE variance is the average squared deviation from it. (Note it divides by $N$, not $N-1$, so it is slightly biased low for small samples.)

$$
\mu^{2} = \frac{1}{N}
$$
## Linear Regression as MLE

### Setting up the probabilistic model

- Data: $\mathcal{D} = \lbrace(\boldsymbol{x}_n, y_n)\rbrace_{n=1}^{N}$, assumed i.i.d.
- Model: $f(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \boldsymbol{\theta}^\top\phi(\boldsymbol{x})$.
- **New assumption:** the output is Gaussian around the model's prediction, with variance 1: $$ p(y_n \mid \boldsymbol{x}_n, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathcal{N}(y_n \mid f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}), 1) $$
- Equivalent generative story: $y_n = f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}) + \varepsilon_n$ with noise $\varepsilon_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. The prediction is the _centre_ of a bell curve, and real observations scatter around it.
- The slide's plot shows the fitted line $f(x;\boldsymbol{\theta})$ with a small Gaussian bump drawn vertically at $x=-2$ and $x=2$, each centred on the line. At every input, $y$ is a bell curve whose centre moves along the line.

Note that we model only the **conditional** $p(y\mid\boldsymbol{x})$, not the distribution of the inputs $\boldsymbol{x}$ themselves. The log-likelihood is $$ \log p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \log \mathcal{N}(y_n \mid f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}), 1) $$

### Deriving the log-likelihood

Recall: $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$, $\log(\exp a) = a$, and "const" means anything that does not depend on $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. Start from the log-likelihood: $$ \log p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \log \mathcal{N}(y_n \mid f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}), 1) $$ Substitute the Gaussian PDF (with $\sigma^2 = 1$): $$ = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \log\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{(y_n - f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}))^2}{2}\right)\right) $$ Apply the log rules to split the product and cancel the log with the exp: $$ = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \left(-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{(y_n - f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}))^2}{2}\right) $$ Pull the $\boldsymbol{\theta}$-free term out as a constant: $$ = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N} (y_n - f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}))^2 + \text{const} $$ The constant $-\frac{N}{2}\log(2\pi)$ has no $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ in it, so it drops out of any optimization over $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.

### MLE = minimizing squared error

Two facts: $\arg\max_a g(a) = \arg\min_a [-g(a)]$, and adding a constant does not move the optimizer, $\arg\min_a g(a) = \arg\min_a [g(a)+\text{const}]$. Then Start from the MLE definition: $$ \boldsymbol{\theta}^{*} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(y_n - f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}))^2 + \text{const}\right] $$ Flip the sign to turn the max into a min: $$ = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(y_n - f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}))^2 - \text{const}\right] $$ Drop the constant, which cannot change the optimizer: $$ = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(y_n - f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta}))^2 $$

> [!success] Key result
>  **MLE with a Gaussian likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the squared error.** The MSE from last week is not an arbitrary choice: it is what you get if you assume the outputs are the model's prediction plus Gaussian noise.

Extra context (beyond the slides):

- The factor $\frac{1}{2}$ and the division by $N$ in MSE are positive constants, so they do not change $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}$.
- Using variance $\sigma^2$ instead of 1 gives $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n (y_n - f)^2 + \text{const}$, and the optimal $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is unchanged. The noise level only rescales the loss.
- **Different noise assumption, different loss.** Laplace noise leads to absolute error (more robust to outliers). Bernoulli outputs lead to cross-entropy, which is the classification setting coming next week.
- Gaussians have thin tails, so squared error punishes large residuals heavily and is **sensitive to outliers**. That is a direct consequence of the Gaussian assumption.

## The General MLE Recipe for Supervised Learning

1. Pick a function form $f$.
2. Pick a conditional distribution $p(y \mid \boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{\theta})$.
3. Find the $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ that makes $\mathcal{D}$ most likely under $p(y \mid \boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{\theta})$.

Compare with last week's recipe: (1) choose a function form relating $\boldsymbol{x}$ to $y$, (2) define a **measurement of loss**, (3) find the function in that class that is "least bad". The probabilistic view replaces the ad hoc loss in step 2 with a modelling assumption about the _distribution_ of $y$, and the loss falls out as the negative log-likelihood. **This recipe will be reused again and again in the course.**

## Summary

- **Likelihood functions** measure how likely an observation (or dataset) is under a given parameter value.
- **MLE** picks the parameter that makes the dataset most likely, usually by maximizing the log-likelihood.
- **Linear regression with a Gaussian likelihood** is equivalent to minimizing the squared error.

## Self-Check Questions

> [!question]- Why do we maximize the log-likelihood instead of the likelihood itself? 
> The log is monotonic, so the maximizer is unchanged. It converts a product over i.i.d. samples into a sum, which avoids numerical underflow and is easier to differentiate.

> [!question]- Can a PDF value exceed 1? Can a PMF value? 
> A PDF can (it is a density, and only its integral must be 1).
>  A PMF cannot, because each value is a genuine probability.

> [!question]- You flip a coin 20 times and see 14 heads. What is the MLE of $P(\text{Head})$? 
> $\theta^{*} = k/N = 14/20 = 0.7$.

> [!question]- Which assumption makes MLE for linear regression equal to least squares? 
> That $y_n$ is Gaussian with mean $f(\boldsymbol{x}_n;\boldsymbol{\theta})$ and a fixed variance (i.e. additive, i.i.d. Gaussian noise).

> [!question]- A dataset has $N$ points and you use a Gaussian with $\mu$ equal to the sample mean. What is the MLE for $\sigma^2$? 
> The average squared deviation from the sample mean, $\frac{1}{N}\sum_n (x_n-\mu^{*})^2$.
