## What is Machine Learning?

Machine learning (ML) shows up whenever we're trying to answer questions like:

1. Given text in a book, what is the likely answer to a question about it?
2. Given previous images, does a new picture contain a car?
3. Given historical drug trials, how effective is a new drug?
4. Given past weather, what will the temperature be in 5 hours?

**Commonality:** all of these are about *recognizing patterns from existing data, so that we can predict on new cases, and make decisions based on those predictions.*

Humans do this constantly, often subconsciously — e.g., "the sky is dark at 1 PM, based on my experience it's going to rain, so I'll bring an umbrella." Machine learning asks: **can we make machines learn patterns, predict, and make decisions the same way?**

### A worked example: predicting gym occupancy

- Input variable $x$ = "time of day", output variable $y$ = "number of people at the gym."
- We have $N$ historical observations (a **dataset**):
$$\mathcal{D} = \{(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_N, y_N)\} = \{(x_n, y_n)\}_{n=1}^N$$
- **Goal:** find a function $f: x \mapsto y$ that fits the data best.
- Given a *new* input $x_{\text{new}}$, predict $y_{\text{new}} = f(x_{\text{new}})$.
- Use the prediction to make a decision — e.g., go to the gym now or later.

This same pattern — collect data, fit a function, predict on new inputs, decide — is the template behind essentially every ML method covered in this course (regression, classification, etc.).

### ML is interdisciplinary

Machine learning sits at the intersection of three fields:

- **Math** — linear algebra and calculus give us the language to describe models and the tools to fit them (e.g., via optimization).
- **Statistics** — probability theory tells us how to reason about uncertainty and noisy data, and how to formalize "best fit."
- **Computer Science** — algorithms and data structures let us actually compute these fits efficiently at scale.

The rest of this note reviews the math prerequisites (functions, vectors, matrices, and basic multivariable calculus) that the rest of the course builds on.

## Math Refresher

### Functions

A function $f$ relates an input $x$ to an output $f(x)$.

- Notation: $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$; $x \mapsto f(x)$, where $\mathcal{X}$ is the **domain** and $\mathcal{Y}$ is the **codomain**.
- $x$ and $f(x)$ can belong to *any* sets — not just numbers.
  - E.g., $\mathcal{X} = \{\text{Alice}, \text{Bob}\}$ and $\mathcal{Y} = \mathbb{R}$ (the real numbers).
  - Then $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ means "takes either Alice or Bob, outputs a real number."

**Examples:**
- $x$: time, $f(x)$: number of people in the gym.
- $x$: a person, $f(x)$: their height.
- $x$: a photo, $f(x)$: whether it contains a car.

This generality matters in ML: inputs are often not single numbers but images, text, or categories, and functions map them to predictions, probabilities, or labels.

In code, a function is exactly the same idea — a mapping from inputs to outputs, just made computable:

```python
def f(x: float) -> float:
    return x * 3.14

def f(x: list[str]) -> str:
    return x[0]
```

### Vectors

A **vector** is a tuple/collection of numbers, usually written as a column:

$$
\boldsymbol{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad
\boldsymbol{a} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.123 \\ 100.99 \\ 0 \\ -8 \end{bmatrix}
$$

A **scalar** is just a single number (or a 1-element vector).

**Notation:**
- $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ means $\boldsymbol{x}$ is a vector of 3 real numbers; $\boldsymbol{a} \in \mathbb{R}^4$.
- $x_i$ denotes the $i$-th component of $\boldsymbol{x}$; e.g., $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = 2$.
- $a_j$ denotes the $j$-th component of $\boldsymbol{a}$; e.g., $a_3 = 0$, $a_4 = -8$.

#### Basic operations

**Vector addition:** for $\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y} \in \mathbb{R}^N$, if $\boldsymbol{z} = \boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{y}$ then $z_i = x_i + y_i$ for every $i = 1, \dots, N$ (component-wise).

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad
$$
**Scalar multiplication:** for $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ and $a \in \mathbb{R}$, if $\boldsymbol{z} = a\boldsymbol{x}$ then $z_i = a x_i$ for every $i$.
$$
\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad
0.1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix}
$$

#### More operations

**Dot product:** for $\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y} \in \mathbb{R}^N$,
$$\boldsymbol{x}^\top \boldsymbol{y} = \sum_{i=1}^N x_i y_i$$
(Both vectors must have the same length $N$; the result is a single scalar.)

**Transpose:** flips a column vector into a row vector (and vice versa). For $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^N$:
$$\boldsymbol{x}^\top = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & \dots & x_N \end{bmatrix}$$

**Norm ($\ell_2$ norm):** measures the "length" or "magnitude" of a vector.
$$\lVert \boldsymbol{x} \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\boldsymbol{x}^\top \boldsymbol{x}}$$

**Worked examples:**
$$
\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}^\top \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = 3\times 3 + 4 \times 4 = 25
\qquad
\left\lVert \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \right\rVert_2 = \sqrt{25} = 5
$$

#### Why vectors are useful

Suppose you're birdwatching and tracking counts of 3 species — "Bald Eagle," "Northern Cardinal," "Snow Owl." Each outing's counts form a vector $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$.

- Previous week: $[3\ \ 25\ \ 1]^\top$ birds.
- This week: $[0\ \ 10\ \ 2]^\top$ birds.
- Total so far (vector addition): $[3\ \ 35\ \ 3]^\top$ birds.

**Key idea:** a vector packages multiple related numbers together so that a *single operation* (like addition) applies to all of them at once. This is exactly how ML represents things like a data point with multiple features (e.g., a house's size, age, and location all in one feature vector).

### Matrices

A **matrix** is a "table" of real numbers with two dimensions:

$$
\boldsymbol{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 \\ 0 & 5.23 & -6 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}
$$

- A matrix has two indices: $A_{ij}$ is the entry in row $i$, column $j$. E.g., $A_{23} = -6$, $A_{11} = 1$.

*(Note: the handout's example labels $A_{11} = -2$; double check against the original slide numbering/positions if this comes up on an assignment — the standard convention is row-then-column indexing as shown above.)*

- **Transpose** of a matrix swaps rows and columns: $\boldsymbol{A}^\top$ has components $A_{ji}$.
$$
\boldsymbol{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 \\ 0 & 5.23 & -6 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}
\implies
\boldsymbol{A}^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 5.23 \\ 3 & -6 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
$$

#### Operations

**Matrix addition and scalar multiplication** work the same as for vectors — component-wise.

**Matrix–vector product:** for $\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N}$ and $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^N$, the product $\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^M$ is defined by
$$(\boldsymbol{Ax})_i = \sum_{k=1}^N A_{ik} x_k$$
i.e., the $i$-th entry of the result is the dot product of row $i$ of $\boldsymbol{A}$ with $\boldsymbol{x}$. Note the inner dimensions must match: $\boldsymbol{A}$'s number of *columns* must equal $\boldsymbol{x}$'s length.

**Example:**
$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 \\ 0 & 5.23 & -6 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 20 \\ -8.85 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Matrix–matrix product:** for $\boldsymbol{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ and $\boldsymbol{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$, the product $\boldsymbol{AB} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times K}$ is defined by
$$(\boldsymbol{AB})_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^N A_{ik} B_{kj}$$

A vector in $\mathbb{R}^N$ can be thought of as simply an $N \times 1$ matrix, so matrix–vector multiplication is a special case of matrix–matrix multiplication.

**Example:**
$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 \\ 0 & 5.23 & -6 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 5 & 8 \\ 6 & 9 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 20 & 46 \\ -8.85 & -49.23 \end{bmatrix}
$$

#### Why matrices are useful

Continuing the birdwatching example: instead of just tracking the running total, record each week's counts separately, then **stack** the weekly vectors into a matrix:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 25 & 1 \\ 0 & 10 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Suppose each bird is worth a certain number of points: $3$ for eagles, $1$ for cardinals, $5$ for owls, i.e., points vector $[3\ \ 1\ \ 5]^\top$. Then each week's total score is obtained with a single matrix–vector product:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 25 & 1 \\ 0 & 10 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 3\times3+25\times1+1\times5 \\ 0\times3+10\times1+2\times5 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 39 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Key idea:** a matrix is a stack of (transposed) vectors, and a matrix–vector product computes a dot product for *every row at once*. This is exactly how ML computes many predictions (one per data point) simultaneously instead of looping over them one at a time.

**Continuing the example — combining norm and scaling:** suppose the scores were earned outdoors (not at a zoo/aviary), so you get a 50% bonus:
$$1.5 \times \begin{bmatrix} 39 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 58.5 \\ 30 \end{bmatrix}$$
To compare against a leaderboard you might want a *single* summary number — this is exactly what a norm gives you:
$$\left\lVert \begin{bmatrix} 58.5 \\ 30 \end{bmatrix} \right\rVert_2 = \sqrt{58.5^2 + 30^2} \approx 65.8$$

> **Remark:** the norm summarizes a vector into a single number by measuring its "length"/"magnitude." This idea reappears constantly in ML — e.g., to measure the size of a model's errors, or how "large" a set of parameters is.

### Vector-valued functions

Functions can take vectors as input and return outputs in any set — including a simple yes/no.

Suppose you get a badge if your total points over two weeks exceed 75. Define $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \{0, 1\}$ where $0$ = "no badge," $1$ = "badge," by:

$$
f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \sum_{i=1}^2 x_i > 75 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

This is an example of an **indicator function**, often written compactly as:
$$f(\boldsymbol{x}) = \mathbb{1}\left[\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^2 x_i > 75\right]$$
where $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ equals $1$ if the condition inside is true, and $0$ otherwise.

**Example:** $f([39, 20]^\top) = 0$ (sum is 59), but $f([58.5, 30]^\top) = 1$ (sum is 88.5) — badge earned!

Indicator functions show up throughout ML, e.g., for defining classification rules or counting how many predictions were correct.

## Review: Multivariable Calculus

### Derivatives

For a function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $x \mapsto f(x)$, the derivative is defined as the limit
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x} = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac{f(x+\varepsilon) - f(x)}{(x+\varepsilon) - x}$$

- The derivative is itself a **function** of $x$ (its value generally changes depending on where you evaluate it).
- $\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x}(x_0)$ evaluates the *rate of change* of $f$ at the specific point $x = x_0$.
- Here $x$ is the variable, while $x_0$ denotes one particular value plugged in for $x$.

### Gradients

Now generalize to functions of several variables: $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, $\boldsymbol{x} \mapsto f(\boldsymbol{x})$.

**Partial derivative** with respect to $x_i$ — the rate of change of $f$ as only $x_i$ varies, holding all other inputs fixed:
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac{f(x_1, \dots, x_i + \varepsilon, \dots, x_n) - f(x_1, \dots, x_i, \dots, x_n)}{(x_i + \varepsilon) - x_i}$$

The **gradient** collects all partial derivatives into a single vector-valued function $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}} f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$:
$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}} f = \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \right]^\top$$

Evaluated at a specific point $\boldsymbol{x}_0$, it becomes a concrete vector:
$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}} f(\boldsymbol{x}_0) = \left[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(\boldsymbol{x}_0), \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}(\boldsymbol{x}_0) \right]^\top$$

> **Important:** $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}} f(\boldsymbol{x}_0)$ points in the direction in which $f$ increases the *most* at $\boldsymbol{x}_0$ (think of vectors as arrows in space).

This fact is the foundation of **gradient descent** and related optimization methods used throughout ML: to *minimize* a function (e.g., a model's error), you repeatedly take small steps in the direction *opposite* the gradient, since that's the direction of steepest *decrease*. This isn't in the slides explicitly yet, but it's the reason gradients matter so much for the rest of the course.

## Summary

- ML is fundamentally about **recognizing patterns from existing data**, in order to **make predictions on new data**, so that we can **make decisions**.
- Doing ML well requires three mathematical pillars: **linear algebra** (vectors, matrices), **calculus** (derivatives, gradients), and **probability** (covered in a later lecture).
- Functions map inputs to outputs across arbitrary sets; vectors and matrices let us represent and manipulate many numbers (e.g., data points, weights) at once; norms summarize vectors into single numbers; gradients tell us the direction of steepest increase of a function, which underlies optimization in ML.
